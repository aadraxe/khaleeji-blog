The Essence of Bahrain: Tradition, Necessity, and Modern Culinary Identity

By Kartheek · afikra, featuring Chef Tala Bashmi

Show name: afkira

Title: afkira

Presenter/chef: Mikey Muhanna featuring Chef Tala Bashmi

Region: Bahrain

Episode title/number: Bahraini Cuisine | Tala Bashmi

Episode Length: 51 minutes

Theme/focus: exploring the historical roots of Bahraini cuisine as a culture built on survival and necessity, and how modern chefs are elevating these traditional Middle Eastern flavors.

Specific Culinary traditions showcased: Seafood preservation techniques (sun-fermenting, drying, salting), Mahyawa (fermented fish paste), Muhammar (fish served on rice for communal sharing), and Goozi (celebratory lamb).

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gGay4zbWOJM

In the afkira episode "Bahraini Cuisine," host Mikey Muhanna interviews Chef Tala Bashmi, a Top Chef finalist and award-winning restaurateur. The episode explores Chef Tala’s journey to modernize Middle Eastern cuisine while staying deeply rooted in Bahrain’s culinary history. Through her insights, the viewer learns that traditional Bahraini food was not born out of luxury, but out of absolute necessity and the demanding physical environment of the island.

Two Questions

  1. As modern chefs elevate traditional, necessity-based Middle Eastern foods into high-end fine dining, do these dishes risk losing their communal, egalitarian origins?

  2. In an island nation where agriculture is heavily restricted by a harsh climate, how do historically imported spices and immigrant cooking techniques redefine the concept of "authentic" local cuisine over generations?

Cultural and Culinary Identity

The episode clearly illustrates how Bahrain’s physical geography shaped its nutritional landscape. As a small island nation with a harsh, dry climate for six months of the year, agricultural output is severely limited (with the exception of hearty winter crops like local tomatoes). Consequently, historical Bahrainis relied heavily on the sea. The traditional foods featured in the episode reflect the demanding, labor-intensive lives of pearl divers and fishermen. Their meals had to be dense, heavy, and rich to provide enough sustenance to last the entire workday.

Because fresh food spoiled quickly in the heat, unique culinary preparations centered on preservation were developed. Chef Tala highlights Mahyawa, a traditional garum (fish sauce). To make it, dried fish is crushed with coriander and cumin, mixed with water, and left to ferment in a glass bottle under the intense Bahraini sun for a month. Another staple, Muhammar, involves cooking a whole fish and shredding it over a large bed of rice.

This preparation highlights the communal eating habits of the region. Historically, Bahrain was not a wealthy island; many families could not afford an entire fish per person. By shredding one fish over a communal platter of rice, the entire family could share a single meal, reinforcing egalitarian dining practices without the need for individual plates or modern utensils. The episode also touches on religious and festive meals, specifically mentioning Goozi (or Qoozi). This dish, consisting of a whole baby lamb cooked over rice, is a rare, cherished meal typically reserved for major Islamic celebrations like Eid.

Finally, the show reveals that Bahrain's modern culinary identity is highly globalized. Current dining trends involve deconstructing nostalgic childhood meals and applying high-end techniques to local ingredients. Furthermore, Chef Tala notes that "authentic" Bahraini food was heavily shaped by Persian, South Asian, and Filipino immigrants who cooked for local families, proving that Middle Eastern cuisine is a dynamic, evolving tapestry rather than a static set of rules.
