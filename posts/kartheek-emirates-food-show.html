How Did the Emirates Evolve Into a Food-Loving Country? A Review of Matbakh

By Kartheek · afikra Matbakh, featuring Chef Sahar Parham Al Awadhi

Title: Matbakh | Food of the Arab World (afikra video podcast)

Chef or presenter's name: Host Mikey Muhanna featuring Chef Sahar Parham Al Awadhi (Emirati Pastry Chef)

Country of origin: United Arab Emirates / Lebanon (afikra Network)

Episode title and number: "How Did the Emirates Evolve Into a Food-Loving Country? | Sahar Parham Al Awadhi"

Length of the episode: 56 minutes

Theme or focus of the episode: The evolution of Dubai's culinary landscape from traditional Bedouin roots to a global fine-dining hub, focusing on how a local Emirati chef creatively translates traditional heritage desserts into sophisticated Michelin-level pastry menus.

Specific culinary traditions showcased: The heavy integration of indigenous dates, local cardamom, and saffron into modern pastry; the influence of the UAE's historical spice import culture; and the communal importance of Khaleeji sweets in hospitality.

Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LaB1ZsasQmE

In the afikra Matbakh episode "How Did the Emirates Evolve Into a Food-Loving Country?", host Mikey Muhanna interviews Sahar Parham Al Awadhi, the first female Emirati chef to work at the iconic Burj Al Arab and the inaugural winner of MENA's Best Pastry Chef award (Muhanna, 2024). The 56-minute documentary-style interview unpacks the complex culinary fabric of Dubai. Al Awadhi provides an insider's perspective on working in the hyper-competitive kitchens of the UAE, contrasting the glittering world of Michelin-starred fine dining with the authentic, unpretentious food that locals actually eat—ranging from traditional Khaleeji family recipes to Sudanese street food. She discusses her personal mission to elevate traditional Emirati flavor profiles by applying classic French pastry techniques to local ingredients.

Two Thought-Provoking Questions

  1. How can modern Emirati chefs balance the commercial necessity of catering to a highly globalized, Michelin-driven tourist market while preserving the authentic, unpretentious essence of traditional Khaleeji cuisine?

  2. In a city like Dubai, which is heavily built on an import culture and a massive expatriate population, to what extent do immigrant street food vendors (such as Sudanese or South Asian cooks) become recognized as a legitimate part of the modern "Emirati" culinary identity?

Cultural and Culinary Identity

The interview profoundly explores how the UAE’s culinary identity is anchored in both its physical and nutritional geography. Geographically, the UAE is characterized by an arid desert climate and a long coastline, historically positioning it as a vital maritime trading port along the ancient spice route (Muhanna, 2024). Because the harsh physical geography limited local agriculture to hardy crops like dates, the historical nutritional geography relied heavily on what could be caught from the Arabian Gulf, milked from camels, or imported from India and Persia. Chef Al Awadhi emphasizes this import culture, noting that the foundation of Emirati food is fundamentally built on imported spices like cardamom, saffron, and dried limes, which were integrated into the local diet centuries ago.

The episode highlights a fascinating dichotomy in Dubai's current food scene. On one hand, current dining trends are dominated by high-end, imported restaurant brands and theatrical molecular gastronomy designed to dazzle tourists. On the other hand, the foundational food of the city consists of hearty Middle Eastern and South Asian staples. Al Awadhi specifically highlights the influence of Sudanese street food and Levantine bakeries that locals frequent. Despite the modernization of the city, traditional Emirati foods remain deeply important. Chef Al Awadhi’s culinary preparations demonstrate a deep respect for heritage; she takes classic, nostalgic Khaleeji flavor profiles—like date syrup and cardamom—and elevates them into sophisticated pastry menus for luxury hotels (Muhanna, 2024).

Furthermore, the discussion touches on traditional eating habits and the role of hospitality. Historically, traditional Emirati meals were eaten communally; diners would sit on the floor around a central platter, using their right hand rather than modern utensils to share food. While Al Awadhi plates her pastries using modern fine-dining utensils, she stresses that the core Emirati eating habit of sharing food remains culturally vital. This is especially true regarding religious and festive meals. During Islamic observances like Ramadan and Eid, the communal sharing of elaborate, heavily spiced meals and rich, date-based sweets is not just a culinary preference, but a profound expression of Islamic charity, unity, and local Bedouin hospitality (Muhanna, 2024). Ultimately, Al Awadhi proves that the UAE's food culture is not just an artificial import, but a continuously evolving tradition.
