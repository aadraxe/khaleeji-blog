Film Reflection · Migration and Memory

Peace by Chocolate: Reflection

By Tanish · On the film Peace by Chocolate (2021)

The Story of the Hadad Family

Peace by Chocolate (2021), tells the true story of the Hadhad family, Syrian refugees who fled the civil war in Damascus and resettled in Antigonish, Nova Scotia, Canada. Before the war, the family had built a thriving chocolate-manufacturing business, which was destroyed in an airstrike. The film opens with the family's arrival in Canada and follows their attempt to rebuild not just a business but a sense of home, dignity, and purpose after losing nearly everything. At the center of the story is the tension between the father, Issam Hadhad, who wants to restart the family chocolate company, and his son Tareq, who had been on the path to becoming a doctor in Syria and is torn between honoring his medical ambitions and supporting his father's dream. What the family is rebuilding, then, is twofold: a livelihood that can sustain them financially and a shared identity that lets them feel like themselves again in an unfamiliar country.

The Scene that Captures the Emotional Reality of Migration

The scene that best illustrates the multifaceted emotional reality of migration is Tareq’s arrival in Halifax. It captures a jarring blend of uncertainty and hope. When he is met by local sponsors Zariah and Heather, the first thing he experiences is the physical shock of the Canadian climate, asking, "Is it that cold?" The grand tour of Antigonish further underscores the uncertainty and loss of his previous life; Tareq, expecting a big city like Toronto or Damascus, is met with a town of only 5,000 people, prompting the quiet, disbelief-filled question: "Where's the town?" However, this scene is later balanced by the shattering emotional weight of loss when Tareq learns that his brother-in-law, Fadi, was killed in Syria. This moment highlights that migration is not just a physical journey but a psychological one where the "lucky" ones carry the trauma of those left behind.

Portrayal of Cultural Adaptation

The film portrays cultural adaptation not as a singular, triumphant milestone, but as a gradual, uneven process marked by systemic, physical, and community challenges. Language presents the most immediate barrier to agency; Issam feels "illiterate" without his son Tareq to translate, suffering a profound loss of professional status after previously running a large enterprise, and facing workplace rejection due to a lack of basic English skills. Physical climate adds another layer of hardship, with the biting Canadian winter requiring a physical and symbolic adjustment—illustrated by the literal effort of shoveling snow, which stands in stark contrast to their life in Damascus. Finally, community integration exhibits subtle friction; while the residents of Antigonish largely extend warm support and financial backing, the Hadhads must navigate bureaucratic hurdles and overcome local skepticism, exemplified by rival business owners who prioritize existing neighbors over newcomers.

Memories from Syria and Their Influence on Decisions in Canada

The memories the Hadhad family carries from Syria are rooted in a mix of professional success, tragic loss, and tradition, all of which directly dictate their actions in Canada. Prior to the war, Issam ran a thriving enterprise with fifty employees; this pride and sense of craftsmanship drove his refusal to allow "Western businessmen" or outside investors to take control of his new venture, as rebuilding the chocolate company serves as a vital way to reclaim his independence and agency after losing everything. The recipes themselves act as an inherited memory and serve as a tangible connection to Syria that allows the family to offer hospitality and bridge cultural divides with their new neighbors. Conversely, Tareq carries the intense memory of attending medical school while Damascus was being bombed, a traumatic past that fuels his fierce determination to finish his degree and regain his identity as a medical professional rather than settling solely into the family trade. Far from being mere nostalgic sentiment, these shared experiences of pride, trauma, and survival shape the family's choices, making them both resilient and fiercely protective of their autonomy as they navigate their new life.

Tensions between Tareq’s Personal Dream and his Family’s Expectations

The tension between Tareq’s personal ambitions and Issam’s familial expectations forms the central conflict of the narrative, illustrating the balance between individual self-actualization and collective survival. Tareq’s pursuit of a medical degree in Syria was abruptly interrupted by the war, driving his intense desire to resume his education in Canada to reclaim his identity as a physician rather than being defined solely by his refugee status or remaining "the waiting son of a chocolate maker". However, his father views the family chocolate business as their shared "light to carry," relying heavily on Tareq’s English fluency and adaptability to navigate Canadian institutions and build their enterprise. Issam perceives Tareq's ambition to leave for medical school as a form of abandonment and ingratitude for the sacrifices made to secure their safety. Rather than framing this clash as a simple moral binary, the narrative treats both perspectives with deep empathy: Tareq represents the universal immigrant drive for individual autonomy, while Issam embodies the refugee's urgent need to preserve cultural identity and ensure family cohesion through a shared legacy.

Personal Reflection: Independence vs Legacy

Reflecting on my own experiences, I find myself relating to both Tareq’s and Issam’s positions but leaning more toward Tareq's search for independence, particularly the desire to define one's own path rather than inherit one automatically. At the same time, I understand Issam's perspective: legacy is not simply stubbornness but a form of continuity, a way of ensuring that everything the family endured was not for nothing. I think most people who have grown up with strong family expectations recognize a version of this tension, even if the stakes are less dramatic than fleeing a war. The film suggests that the two impulses are not actually opposites; Tareq eventually finds a way to apply his own skills and ambition toward the family's shared project, which is less a surrender of independence than a redefinition of it.

What held the Family together

Despite conflict, displacement, and disagreement, what keeps the Hadhad family together is a combination of shared history, mutual sacrifice, and an underlying commitment to one another that outlasts any individual dispute. The chocolate business becomes a symbol of this unity: even when family members disagree about strategy or priorities, they are still working toward something they built together. The film also emphasizes ritual and food as unifying forces, since shared meals and shared work in the kitchen repeatedly bring the family back into alignment after moments of tension. Ultimately, it is not the absence of conflict that holds the family together but their willingness to keep choosing each other despite it.

Film’s suggestion about Immigrant Business Survival

The film suggests that the survival and growth of immigrant-owned businesses depend on a powerful synergy between community backing, uncompromising product quality, and strategic adaptability. Community support serves as a crucial springboard, illustrated by the interest-free $12,000 loan raised by the residents of Antigonish and the hands-on assistance from volunteers—a turning point that provides vital capital without violating the family's faith or overwhelming them with traditional bank constraints. However, goodwill alone is insufficient; it must be matched by the family's relentless persistence and commitment to excellence. Issam’s refusal to compromise on premium Syrian ingredients or his traditional craftsmanship, even while working out of a small shed with a simple ladle, ensures the product stands out. Furthermore, the Hadhads leverage the power of authentic storytelling, transforming "Peace by Chocolate" into a message of hope and resilience that resonates deeply with local consumers, media, and national leaders alike. Ultimately, business survival is portrayed not merely as a matter of individual grit, but as a reciprocal relationship where newcomers adapt to local regulations and collaborate with their hosts, while the host community actively invests in the newcomers' success.

Moment in the Film that Resonates

The moment of personal resonance centers on the emotional power of food and shared meals as universal catalysts for human connection and belonging. In the narrative, watching the Hadhad family offer their handmade Syrian chocolate to the wider Antigonish community—and later reuniting around a traditional family dinner featuring Shahnaz's muhammara—illustrates how food serves as a portable architecture of memory. For those who have lost their physical homeland, preserving culinary traditions allows them to keep their cultural identity alive while building a bridge to win over skeptical neighbors. Sharing a dish or a handcrafted sweet communicates warmth, gratitude, and diplomacy far more effectively than words alone, capturing how shared meals operate simultaneously as personal comfort, cultural pride, and a welcoming invitation across differences.

Reflecting on: “Feed to empower, feed to survive, feed to connect, feed to control.”

This framework maps clearly onto the film's portrayal of food. The Hadhads feed to survive in the most literal sense during their displacement and resettlement, relying on both their own resourcefulness and community support. They feed to connect as they share chocolate with neighbors, using food to bridge cultural gaps and build trust in an unfamiliar place. They feed to empower as the chocolate business becomes a source of income, independence, and renewed purpose, allowing the family to reclaim a sense of control over their own future. The idea of feeding to control is more subtle in the film, but it appears in the way food and business become a means of asserting agency after a period when the family had almost none, using the act of feeding others as a way of reasserting their place in the world rather than simply receiving charity.

How the Film Deepens my Understanding on the Refugee Experience

Watching Peace by Chocolate deepened my understanding of the refugee experience by showing it as an ongoing process rather than a single dramatic event. The film resists the temptation to end the story at the moment of arrival, instead following the slower, less cinematic work of rebuilding a life: learning a language, navigating unfamiliar systems, repairing family relationships strained by trauma, and finding a sustainable way to contribute to a new community. It challenged any tendency to think of refugees primarily through the lens of crisis, showing instead their skills, ambitions, and ordinary hopes. It also highlighted the role that receiving communities play, not as passive backdrops but as active participants whose willingness to extend trust and support can meaningfully shape whether a family is able to thrive. Overall, the film left me with a more nuanced view of migration as a long, collaborative process of rebuilding rather than a single moment of rescue.
