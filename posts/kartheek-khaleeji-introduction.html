Looking Past the Skyline: Why I Chose the Khaleeji Region

By Kartheek

Whenever I see the Khaleeji region portrayed in media or online, the focus is always on rapid modernization, futuristic skyscrapers, and raw wealth. To be honest, that flashy exterior is not what drew me to this project. I chose to explore this culture because I want to look past the skyline and understand the people themselves, particularly their Bedouin roots and coastal heritage. I keep wondering how a society can navigate such an intense pace of development while still trying to hold onto its core identity, while other places seem to have lost themselves in the process. To me, food feels like the most honest and grounding way to figure that out. Buildings and skylines can transform an entire landscape in twenty years, but culinary traditions seem to stick around a lot longer.

One aspect I am curious about is how deeply migration and historical trade routes shaped their everyday kitchen. Because the Arabian Gulf was a maritime trading hub, it spent centuries absorbing influences from South Asia, Persia, and East Africa. It is interesting to think about how living in a desert and coastal climate may have caused early communities to look outward. They turned foreign goods like cardamom, saffron, and sun-dried black limes into the exact ingredients that now define what home tastes like to them. It shows how physical place and trade routes built their identity from the ground up, proving that what we consider authentic tradition is often born out of survival and cultural exchange.

If I had to share or celebrate this culture, I would focus on the rituals that bring people together, specifically the coffee ceremony with Gahwa and communal dishes like Machboos. Gahwa, which is lightly roasted coffee brewed with cardamom and served alongside sweet dates. It is an expression of Karam, the deep cultural commitment to hospitality. Everything from the hand you use to pour it to the rhythm of refilling the cup is tied to respect, honoring elders, and welcoming guests. Gathering around a shared platter of Machboos works the exact same way. Eating together from one central dish breaks down barriers and makes mealtime completely communal, similarly to the Moroccan culture we’ve been learning about.

As our group dives deeper into this project, I really hope we can explore how modern families are keeping these traditions alive. With so much globalization and massive expat populations across Gulf cities, I wonder if younger generations still spend hours making slow cooked traditional dishes, or if their relationship with food is shifting toward convenience.
