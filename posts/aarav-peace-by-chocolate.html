Film Reflection · Migration and Memory

What We Carry With Us: Peace by Chocolate and the Sweetness of Starting Over

By Aarav · On the film Peace by Chocolate (2021)

I went into Peace by Chocolate expecting a feel-good movie about a small business. I came out thinking about my own family. On the surface the film is about a Syrian family that opens a chocolate shop in a small Canadian town, but the longer I sat with it, the more I realized it is really about migration, memory, family, and the quiet negotiations we make between who we are and who we want to become.

A Factory in Damascus, a Kitchen in Antigonish

The Hadhad family ran a thriving chocolate factory in Damascus with dozens of employees before the Syrian civil war. When the factory is bombed, they lose almost everything and are forced to flee. After years of displacement, they are sponsored to resettle in Antigonish, a small town in Nova Scotia. What they rebuild there is not just a business but a whole life. They start making chocolate again in a home kitchen and slowly grow it into the company Peace by Chocolate. To me the chocolate was never only about money. It was about getting back a version of themselves that the war had taken away.

Snow as a Story

The scene that stayed with me most was the family's arrival in snowy, rural Nova Scotia. They come from a warm, busy city and suddenly they are standing in a cold, quiet, completely unfamiliar place. The film does not rush past that moment, so you feel all of it at once: relief that they made it, uncertainty about how to belong, grief for everything left behind, and a small, fragile hope that something can be built here. I liked that the movie showed all of this through something as simple as snow instead of a big dramatic speech. The climate almost becomes its own character, a constant reminder of how far they are from home.

The town is shown honestly too. Some locals, especially their sponsor Frank, are warm and generous, while others are cold or unwelcoming. Language creates awkward moments and some gentle humor, but the film never makes fun of the family for it. That honesty made it feel real.

A Son, a Father, and a Business

The heart of the film is the tension between Tareq, the son, and Issam, his father. Tareq dreams of becoming a doctor. Issam wants him close, focused on the family and the chocolate. Issam still carries his identity as a master chocolatier, and that memory is exactly why he insists on rebuilding chocolate instead of starting something new. The trauma of the bombing, and the pain of leaving Tareq's sister behind in Syria, makes the family protective of one another. When you have already lost that much, a son leaving does not feel like normal growing up. It feels like one more loss.

If I am honest, I lean toward Tareq. A legacy that asks someone to give up their own future is not really preservation, it is more like a cage. But I understand Issam too, because his need to hold on to the business is really grief in disguise. He is protecting the one thing the war could not destroy. The ending suggests the two are not opposites, and that you can honor your family without erasing yourself.

Down Memory Lane

That push and pull brought me right back to when I first decided to move to the US to study. My parents were not very much for the idea at first, because they were scared about whether I would be able to manage everything on my own. Like Issam, their hesitation came from fear, not a lack of love. Over time they saw how much the move could give me: new things to learn, new ways of life, and a much wider view of the world. Watching Tareq reminded me that a parent's resistance is often just worry wearing a different face.

Feeding to Survive, Connect, Empower, and Control

In class we used the framework "feed to survive, feed to connect, feed to empower, feed to control," and it fits the Hadhads almost perfectly. They feed to survive at first, because chocolate is income. They feed to connect as their chocolate becomes a bridge to the town. They feed to empower as the business restores their pride. And in a quieter way they feed to control, since Issam partly uses the business to keep Tareq close.

This film connects directly to our project on the Gulf. Dubai is a city built by migration, where most residents come from somewhere else, and many of them, like the Hadhads, bring their food with them as a way of carrying home. Before this movie, I mostly saw refugee stories as stories of need and rescue. Peace by Chocolate reframed it for me as a story about what people bring with them, and that shift has stayed with me.
