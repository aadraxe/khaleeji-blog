Restaurant Visit Reflection

Sitting Low, Eating Together: Lunch at Abu Naji

By Tanish · Abu Naji, Busch Blvd, Tampa

I expected a normal lunch out where we would try some cuisine and then reflect on the experience. What I got felt closer to being welcomed into someone's home. Our group chose Abu Naji, a Yemeni restaurant on Busch Boulevard, because our project focuses on Khaleeji cuisine, and Yemen's food traditions overlap heavily with its Gulf neighbors. Aarav, Kartheek, and my friend Sarthak came along, and none of us knew quite what to expect.

The Room Tells the Story First

Before we even looked at a menu, the room was introducing us to Yemen. Red woven fabric with black and white geometric patterns covered the walls, much like the Gulf's traditional Al Sadu weaving. A framed photo of Cairo Castle in Taiz hung nearby, and a small model of a Yemeni tower house sat on a shelf with brass figures and woven baskets. Even the ceiling was carved and decorated. With Arabic music playing quietly, I felt like the owners wanted us to experience Yemen, not just order from it.

No Chairs, No Personal Space

The biggest surprise was the seating. In addition to tables, there was a large patterned carpet surrounded by low, cushioned sofas, with all the food set in the middle. We chose to sit on the floor to get the full experience and in doing so nobody had a private plate to retreat to. If you wanted the pita, you reached across, or someone passed it to you. It was a little awkward for the first few minutes, but then the awkwardness faded and the conversation got louder and easier. Seeing how a seating arrangement can change how people talk to one another was my biggest takeaway from the night.

Tasting Without Assumptions

We shared hummus, baba ghanoush, fattoush, and fries. I'll admit my first thought about the dips was that they were mild. Then we looked into it and learned that both dishes come from the Levant and are meant to get their flavor from tahini, lemon, garlic, and roasted eggplant, not heavy spice. So the restaurant was being authentic, and I was the one with the wrong expectations. The fattoush, crunchy and tangy, was a nice contrast to the creamy dips.

Kartheek ordered the chicken mabdi, which arrived on a raised platter with yellow rice and chicken with crisp, charred skin. The menu also listed mandi, and we wondered what set them apart. The answer is technique: mandi is slow cooked in a pit or clay oven, while mabdi is grilled on hot stones over charcoal.

A Conversation Across Borders

When we talked with a staff member, he told us he wasn't specifically from Yemen but was still from the Khaleeji region. Dishes like mandi, lamb and rice, chicken and rice are everyday food across the Gulf, so here was a Yemeni meal in Tampa, served by someone from a neighboring country. Food clearly doesn't stay inside borders. It travels with people and becomes shared.

What I'm Taking With Me

I came for a class project and left thinking about how much a meal can say. The walls, the carpet, the shared platters, and the people serving us all carried culture in a way a menu alone never could. Eating together on the floor reminded me that the simplest way to understand someone's tradition is to sit down and share it.
