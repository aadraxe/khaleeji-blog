In-Class Question · What Is It About Food That Brings Us Together?

Half of My Lunch: What Sharing Food Teaches Us About Belonging

By Aarav

For as long as I can remember, I have been sharing my lunch with my friends. It started back in school, trading bites across the table and offering half of whatever I had brought that day. I never thought much about it then, but looking back, those small exchanges taught me more about the people around me than almost anything else. You learn a lot about someone from what they love to eat, what they miss from home, and what they are willing to try. Sharing food made it easier to understand my friends, and easier to grow close to them.

One of the Oldest Habits We Have

When our professor asked the class what it is about food that brings us together, I realized this is not just my story. Sharing food is one of the oldest threads in the human story. Archaeologists have found hearths hundreds of thousands of years old where people gathered to eat side by side. In the ruins left by Vesuvius, a loaf of bread was uncovered already scored into sections, baked to be broken and passed around. Someone almost two thousand years ago made bread with sharing built into its shape.

To break bread with someone is to set anger aside, to make room for laughter, and to say without words that you belong here. Maybe that is why food marks every moment that matters, from birthdays and weddings to the dishes we cook to remember the people we have lost.

A Circle of Chairs

This semester gave me a lot of chances to test that idea. During our msemmen and Moroccan tea demonstration, we pulled our chairs into a circle around the kitchen island instead of sitting in rows. Everyone was stretching dough, making mistakes, and laughing about it, all working toward the same goal. By the end of that afternoon I felt more connected to my classmates than I had all semester, and nobody had given a speech about teamwork. The food did that on its own.

The tea taught the same lesson from the host's side. In Moroccan culture, tea is always offered to guests, and pouring it well is a sign of respect. In India, my family serves a drink and snacks to every guest who walks in. In the Gulf, guests are welcomed with gahwa and dates, and there are customs for how the cup is poured and held. Three different cultures, and all of them say the same thing first: you are welcome here, have something to drink.

Sitting on the Floor in Tampa

The clearest example came when our group visited Abu Naji, a Yemeni restaurant in Tampa. Instead of tables and chairs, we sat on low cushions around a big patterned carpet, majlis style, with the food in the middle. We realized it had been years since any of us had sat on the floor to share a meal. In most restaurants, everyone gets their own chair and their own plate. On the carpet, we were reaching across each other, passing pita, and trying from the same dishes. The conversation was different too, more relaxed and more like a family gathering than a group project.

That is what the majlis is designed for. In the Gulf, shared platters of rice like machboos come from a history where food was scarce and one large pot had to stretch across many people. Sharing was not just polite, it was how everyone got fed. That history still shapes how people eat together today.

Back to the Lunch Table

After everything I learned this semester, from Moroccan tea glasses to Yemeni platters to Gullah Geechee rice pots, I keep coming back to the same simple picture: a lunch table and a friend reaching for something on my plate. That small act, repeated over years, is how many of my closest friendships began. Food brings us together because it asks us to trust each other a little, to try something new, and to make room at the table. Every culture we studied had its own way of saying that, but they were all saying it.
