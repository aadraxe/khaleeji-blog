Class Discussion · Food, Heritage and Cultural Identity

Recipes That Crossed Oceans: The Many Kitchens Behind American Food

By Aarav · From our class discussion on food and identity within diverse American communities

When I first moved to the US, I assumed "American food" meant burgers and fries. Our class discussion on food and identity showed me how wrong that was. American food is not one tradition but many, shaped by communities that carried their cooking across oceans, borders, and generations. Once I started tracing those stories, every plate began to look like a map.

Rice From the Other Side of the Atlantic

The example that moved me most was the Gullah Geechee community of the coastal Carolinas and Georgia. The Gullah Geechee people descend from enslaved West Africans taken from places such as Senegal and Angola and brought to the Low Country to grow Carolina Gold rice. Their cooking keeps West African traditions alive through dishes like red rice, okra soup, and Hoppin' John.

What struck me was the role of geography. Red rice comes from West African cooking, and because the coastal landscape of West Africa resembles the Gullah Geechee Corridor, it was easier for the community to keep many of their food traditions going. The land itself helped them remember. Hoppin' John, which Gullah Geechee tradition says brings good luck on New Year's Day, combines West African ingredients like black-eyed peas, rice, and greens with North American additions such as thyme, celery, and corn bread. One pot holds two continents, which makes Gullah Geechee cuisine a living link between Africa and the American South.

The People Who Laid the Foundation

The foundation of American cuisine starts with Native American peoples. In Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) villages and many other Native communities, women grew corn, beans, and squash, known as the Three Sisters. Beans climb the cornstalks, squash spreads below to block weeds, and cooked together they provide nearly complete nutrition. I love that the crops literally support each other, because it is the same idea as a shared meal.

African influence is just as central. Skilled Black cooks shaped Southern food, and eventually American food, even though they have largely been left out of history. The term "soul food" came out of the Black Power movement of the 1960s as a way to name food that reflected African American history. European settlers brought wheat breads, dairy, and preserved meats, German immigrants shaped hot dogs, and Italian immigrants turned pizza and pasta into national favorites.

Chop Suey and the Art of Adapting

The Chinese American story taught me the most about survival. Facing xenophobia and restrictive immigration laws, Chinese immigrants adapted their cooking for new customers and new ingredients, creating dishes like chop suey and General Tso's chicken. They grew out of Chinese cooking but became distinctly American. Mexican, Vietnamese, Korean, Indian, and Caribbean communities keep adding layers to what Americans eat every day.

What Distance Does to a Dish

The question I chose to answer in class was how moving to a new country changes the way a person values the food of their homeland. For me, the answer is that it makes home food more meaningful, not less. When you live at home, traditional meals are ordinary. They are just what gets served on a Tuesday night. After moving abroad, those same meals become rare and hard to recreate, and that scarcity turns them into symbols of family, memory, and belonging.

Food is tied to the senses in a way few other things are. A smell from a kitchen can bring back a grandmother's house or a holiday faster than any photograph. For immigrants and international students like me, cooking a familiar meal becomes a way of protecting identity in a place where language, customs, and routines are all different.

At the same time, living abroad creates a tension between holding on and adapting. Many immigrants swap ingredients because the originals are expensive or unavailable, and over time those changes become traditions of their own, just like chop suey. Food identity is not fixed. It is protected and reshaped at the same time. We later saw this exact pattern in the Gulf, where Yemeni mandi became everyday food across the region and ended up being served to us in Tampa. Distance teaches you that food carries heritage, and sharing a dish from home with new friends becomes a way of explaining who you are without needing many words.
