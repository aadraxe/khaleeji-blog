Media: Music

The Sound of the Gulf: From Pearl Boats to Pop

By Tanish

While researching Khaleeji food, I kept running into the same history from a different angle: music. The pearl divers, Bedouin poets and traders who shaped what people eat in the Gulf also shaped what they sang. Following the music turned out to be another way to follow the region’s story, from the sea to the desert to today’s pop charts.

Songs of the sea

Before oil, life on the coast revolved around fishing, pearling and sea trade, and it had its own music, known as fann al-bahri, or sea music. Work songs were once the most important part of it. Every task on a pearling boat, like pulling up the anchor, had its own song and rhythm, so sailors knew a huge repertoire. Scholars who recorded this music called the pearl divers’ songs “the most refined music-making of the area” (Qatar Digital Library).

Fjiri

The leisure side of sea music is fjiri. It was performed at the start and end of the pearling season and in meeting houses called the dar in Bahrain and Qatar, or the diwaniya in Kuwait. Fjiri grew out of pearl diving in Muharraq, Bahrain, in the 19th century. Musicians play finger chimes, drums and a clay pot called a jahl, sitting in a circle around a lead singer and dancers. Bahrain’s Ismeal Dawas band still practices every Wednesday evening, and fjiri joined UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list in December 2021 (The National).

Sawt: the city sound

Sawt grew up in the small coastal towns of Kuwait and Bahrain. It mixes Bedouin and fishing-community traditions with music from Yemen, Saudi Arabia and Egypt. A singer plays the oud, a pear-shaped lute, backed by a violin and the mirwas, a small double-headed drum, combining classical Arabic scales with Gulf rhythms and poetry (Qatar Digital Library).

Laywa: rhythms from East Africa

Some Gulf music crossed the sea on the same trade routes as spices. Laywa traces back to the East African coast, mainly Tanzania and Kenya. A surnay, a kind of oboe, leads while dancers circle around it, backed by a group of drums and percussion (Qatar Digital Library). Like loomi from Persia or rice dishes from India, laywa shows that Khaleeji culture was built from many places.

Today: Khaleeji pop

After pearling declined and steamships replaced sailing boats, many sea songs stopped being performed. Today they survive in the dar and diwaniya, in government-supported folk groups, and on rescued old recordings. Meanwhile, Gulf music moved into studios. Hussain Al Jassmi, from the UAE, is one of the biggest names in modern Khaleeji music, and “Bawada’ak” (2001) was the song that made him a star (The National).

My takeaway: Going from fjiri to Al Jassmi is a quick way to hear the jump from the pearling economy to the oil economy. Just like the food, the music did not disappear when the region modernized. It moved into new places, from boat decks to meeting houses to a UNESCO list to the radio.
