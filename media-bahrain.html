Food Show Review 2 · Bahrain

Bahraini Cuisine with Tala Bashmi

By Aarav, Kartheek and Tanish

Our second Matbakh episode takes us to Bahrain, the island kingdom that was once the pearling center of the Gulf (see our region page). After the UAE episode, it was interesting to see how a smaller, poorer island built its own food identity. Each of us wrote our own review.

The episode

Matbakh · Guest: Tala Bashmi · Bahrain · about 50 minutes

Aarav’s review: a cuisine built on necessity

Tala Bashmi, named MENA’s Best Female Chef in 2022 and chef patron of Fusions by Tala in Manama, describes Bahraini food as a cuisine born of necessity. Its people were fishermen and pearl divers, not wealthy, so food had to stretch and last: salted and dried shrimp and fish, and one whole fish shredded over rice so everyone gets a share. That is the same “feed many from little” idea we saw in machboos.

Her chosen ingredient is mehyawa, a fermented fish sauce made from dried fish, coriander, cumin and salt, left in the sun for about a month and eaten on bread with a fried egg and cheese. For celebrations there is ghoozi, baby lamb over rice for Eid, the dish she missed most while studying abroad. As an international student, I related to that a lot.

Questions it raised for me: Bashmi says Bahraini palates were shaped by the Indian, Filipino and Persian workers who cooked in many homes. How should a national cuisine acknowledge the migrant hands that shaped it? And if Bahraini food was built on scarcity and sharing, can it move into luxury fine dining without losing that meaning?

Tanish’s review: resilience on a tiny island

What stood out to me is how closely Bahrain’s identity is tied to being a “tiny island.” The extreme heat makes growing produce hard for much of the year (though tomatoes do well in winter), so seafood and preservation became the base of the diet. Bashmi’s dishes show how food was designed around affordability as much as flavor: in muhammar, one fish is shredded over rice so a whole family can share it, while guzi, baby lamb over rice, turns Eid into a celebration. I also liked how she is moving the cuisine forward with playful dishes like a deconstructed peanut butter and jelly sandwich and her take on the “Matilda cake,” using advanced techniques to recreate childhood joy.

Questions it raised for me: How might Bahraini cuisine have developed if the country had not depended so heavily on seafood and preservation? And as Bahrain becomes more globally connected, how can traditional foods stay important without losing their original meaning?

Kartheek’s review: sharing without plates

What stood out to me is how muhammar shows the egalitarian side of Bahraini dining. Many families could not afford a whole fish per person, so one fish shredded over a communal platter of rice let everyone share the same meal without individual plates. Bashmi also calls mahyawa Bahrain’s garum, a fish sauce fermented in a glass bottle under the sun for a month. Most interesting to me was her point that “authentic” Bahraini food was shaped by Persian, South Asian and Filipino cooks, which shows that Middle Eastern cuisine is an evolving mix rather than a fixed set of rules.

Questions it raised for me: As chefs turn necessity-based dishes into fine dining, do those dishes lose their communal origins? And in an island where farming is so limited, how do imported spices and immigrant techniques change what counts as “authentic” local food over generations?

What this episode taught us: Bahraini food was shaped by a harsh climate, the sea, and the migrant cooks who fed its families. The same pearl divers who ate this food also sang fjiri, and their “feed many from little” idea lives on in dishes like machboos.
