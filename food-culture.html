Food and Culture

Coffee, Sweets and Rice: Three Foods That Bring the Gulf Together

By Kartheek

Seven countries, one sea, and a lot of history. So what actually ties the Khaleeji region together? When our group compared what people eat from Kuwait to Oman, the answer kept coming back to three foods: a cup of coffee, a plate of fried sweets, and a big tray of spiced rice. Each one carries a different part of Gulf culture: hospitality, celebration, and community.

Before you eat: how to be a good guest

Food in the Gulf comes with manners, and most of them are about respect and sharing (Abu Dhabi Department of Culture and Tourism):

  • Use your right hand to take and return your coffee cup.
  • Elders first: the oldest or most important guest is served first.
  • One to three cups: coffee is poured only a quarter full, and it is polite to drink at least one cup but not more than three.
  • Share the platter: food is set in the middle and eaten together, often sitting on the floor in the majlis, the family sitting room.

We got to try that last one ourselves at Abu Naji, and it changed the whole feel of the meal. You can read about it in our restaurant reflections.

Gahwa: the coffee of welcome

Gahwa is Arabic coffee, and it is nothing like the coffee I grab between classes. The beans are roasted, ground and brewed with cardamom, then poured from a dallah, a tall pot with a long curved spout, into tiny cups called finjal. It is served at every kind of occasion, from business meetings to weddings, and it stands for generosity and unity.

In 2015, Arabic coffee was added to UNESCO’s list of Intangible Cultural Heritage after a joint nomination by the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman and Qatar (Abu Dhabi Department of Culture and Tourism). For me, that detail says a lot: four countries agreed that one cup of coffee represents all of them.

Luqaimat: the sweet of celebration

Luqaimat means “little bites.” They are small balls of dough flavored with saffron and cardamom, deep fried until golden, and drizzled with date syrup (dibs) or honey, so they are crisp outside and soft inside (HIDMC). They are sold fresh in markets during Ramadan and made for Eid, National Day and family gatherings.

What I like most is the date syrup. Dates were the energy food Bedouins carried through the desert, and they are still at the center of this celebration sweet. If you have had Indian gulab jamun or Greek loukoumades, luqaimat is a close cousin: fried dough and syrup show up in celebrations all over the world.

Machboos: the dish that feeds everyone

Machboos is the rice dish of the Gulf. Rice, meat or fish and spices are cooked in one pot so the rice soaks up all the flavor. The key ingredient is loomi, dried black lime, along with cardamom and baharat, a Gulf spice mix. It is regarded as the national dish of Bahrain (National Geographic) and of Kuwait (Remitly).

Machboos is served on one large platter in the middle, and everyone eats from it together. That connects straight back to the Bedouin idea of feeding many from little. It is also a trade story: the rice and most of the spices arrived by boat from India and Persia.

None of these foods is meant to be eaten alone. That is the core of Khaleeji food identity as I understand it now: sharing and hospitality.

Keeping tradition alive

These traditions are under pressure. Researchers found that harees, a wheat and meat dish once made only for Ramadan and weddings, is now eaten year round, and that in many Emirati homes traditional dishes are now cooked by hired cooks from other countries (The National). At the same time, governments are protecting what they can, from UNESCO listings to rebuilt markets. My conclusion: the dishes are changing, but the values behind them are what people fight hardest to keep.
