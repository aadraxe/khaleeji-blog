Class Reflections

What We Learned in the Classroom and Beyond

Led by Kartheek, with Aarav

Context: IDH 4200, Geographical Perspectives

This blog is part of our Honors course, IDH 4200 Geographical Perspectives, which prepares us for the Global Citizenship Project. Through readings, class discussions, interviews, restaurant visits and our classmates’ presentations, we practiced engaging with other cultures with competence and humility, using food and culinary traditions as the way in. Below are our notes from the class discussion on food and identity and from the presentations of other groups.

Aarav: Food and Identity discussion

Written for our class discussion on food and identity, which asked how food shapes culture and heritage.

Culinary traditions of diverse American communities

American food is not one tradition but many, shaped by the communities that carried their cooking across oceans, borders and generations. One strong example is the Gullah Geechee community of the coastal Carolinas and Georgia, descended from enslaved West Africans brought to the Low Country to grow Carolina Gold rice (National Geographic). Their cooking keeps West African traditions alive through dishes like red rice, okra soup and Hoppin’ John. Red rice comes from West African cooking, and because the coastal landscape of West Africa resembles the Gullah Geechee Corridor, the community was able to continue many African food traditions (National Park Service).

Cultures that founded and influenced American cuisine

American cuisine begins with Native American peoples, who grew corn, beans and squash, known as the Three Sisters (Smithsonian Center for Folklife and Cultural Heritage). Skilled Black cooks shaped Southern food, and eventually American food, even though they have largely been left out of history (VOA Learning English). European, Mexican and Asian immigrants added more layers, and Chinese immigrants facing restrictive laws created dishes like chop suey that became distinctly American (PBS).

My question: how does moving abroad change the way we value food from home?

Moving to a new country often makes food from home more meaningful, not less. At home, traditional meals are ordinary. Abroad, they become rare and hard to recreate, and that scarcity turns them into symbols of family, memory and belonging. Many immigrants adapt recipes with local ingredients, and over time those adaptations become new traditions of their own. Food identity is not fixed. It is protected and reshaped at the same time, which is exactly what we later saw in the Gulf.
