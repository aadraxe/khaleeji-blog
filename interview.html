“They Feel Like They’re Back Home”: Our Interview at Abu Naji

Interview by Kartheek, Tanish and Aarav · Transcript edited by Aarav

During our visit to Abu Naji, a Yemeni restaurant on Busch Boulevard in Tampa, we sat down on the majlis seating with a staff member who told us he is from the Khaleeji region. We talked about the restaurant, how they welcome guests, what Ramadan looks like there, and how food connects cultures. His answers summed up a lot of what our whole project is about.

We are sharing this interview as text only, at the interviewee’s request. His answers below are edited for clarity and length.

The answers that stood out to us

A piece of home

What’s the story behind the restaurant?

“A lot of people come here because our food and the place are like back home, like Yemen. We have the floor seating, plus a private area for families to have coffee and breakfast. Our food has the flavor of back home, so when people come, they feel like they’re back home. That’s the best thing. Everything is fresh here, the lamb, the chicken.”

What should a first-time guest order?

“Start with the mandi. The meat is slow cooked until it is very soft, and the rice takes all the flavor. If you like it smoky, try the mabdi, grilled on stones. And share it. One big plate in the middle for everybody, that is how we eat at home.”

The guest comes first

How do you welcome guests here?

“First, we bring coffee or tea and dates, before anything else. In our culture, the guest comes first. That is why we have the floor seating too. People sit together, they take their time, nobody rushes them.”

How can food connect cultures and communities?

“We see a lot of families and events here. Every country has its own food, its own style, but Yemeni food is for everybody, because it’s lamb and rice. Everybody eats lamb and rice, so it’s easy for everybody to eat. Any culture, even us as Arabs, we all eat rice and chicken.”

Tradition is changing, not disappearing

What changes here during Ramadan?

“Ramadan is our busiest time. Families come just before sunset and break the fast together with dates and water, then soup, then the big plates of rice and meat. It feels like one big family in here.”

Do younger people still eat this food?

“Yes, but it is changing. The young ones like burgers and pizza too, but when they come with their parents, they order mandi. Food is how they stay connected to where their family comes from.”

What we learned

Three ideas from this conversation stayed with us. First, the restaurant is not only a business but a piece of home: the food, the majlis seating and the family coffee area are there so that people from Yemen and the Gulf “feel like they’re back home.” That matches what we wrote in our restaurant reflections about the room telling a story before the food arrives.

Second, hospitality comes before the meal. Coffee and dates for every guest is the same tradition we described in gahwa, and his answer about lamb and rice explains why dishes like machboos and mandi travel so well. Rice and meat are shared across Arab, South Asian and many other cuisines, so a Yemeni platter feels familiar to almost anyone who sits down at it.

Third, his answer about younger people is our project’s big question in one sentence. The food is changing, but families still use it to stay connected to where they come from, the same “evolution, not modernizing” idea we heard in our food show reviews.
