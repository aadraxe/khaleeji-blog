Food Show Review 1 · United Arab Emirates

How Did the Emirates Evolve Into a Food-Loving Country?

By Aarav, Kartheek and Tanish

Reading about a culture only gets you so far. For our food show reviews, our group watched two episodes of Matbakh, afikra’s series on the food of the Arab world, where host Mikey Muhanna talks with chefs about the history behind their cuisine. This first episode covers the UAE; the second covers Bahrain. Each of us wrote our own review.

The episode

Matbakh, Episode 8 · Guest: Sahar Parham Al Awadhi · United Arab Emirates · about 48 minutes

Aarav’s review

Sahar Parham Al Awadhi is an Emirati pastry chef who worked at the Burj Al Arab and was named the Middle East and North Africa’s Best Pastry Chef. She explains that the area was historically poor, so its cooking borrowed from Iran, Oman, Saudi Arabia, Yemen and India, which matched our own research.

The traditional foods were my favorite part: rangina, fresh dates covered in roasted flour, sugar and cardamom; regag, a crispy crepe spread with mahyawa, a fermented fish paste; and luqaimat soaked in date syrup. She says Dubai is “forming its own culinary DNA” and prefers the word “evolution” over “modernizing” for how Emirati food should change, which became a theme for our whole project.

Questions it raised for me: If Gulf countries share nearly identical dishes under different names, who decides what counts as a “national” cuisine? And as Emirati chefs bring French pastry techniques into traditional sweets, how can they refine these dishes without losing the family memories that give them meaning?

Tanish’s review: evolution, not modernization

What stood out to me is that Al Awadhi, the first Emirati pastry chef at the Burj Al Arab, sees Dubai as far more than the “glitz and glam” of the tourist strip. Her “onion” approach to sourcing, local farms first, then the GCC, then the world, shows how a global port can still cook locally. I was also struck by the eating habits: at seafood restaurants, guests still eat local fish with their hands over rice, and her “Um Ali 2.0” reclaims a wedding dessert she disliked as a child to give that memory “a better ending.”

Questions it raised for me: As the UAE moves from a protein-and-grain “cuisine of necessity” to a more vegetable-rich diet, how will that change the identity of future Emirati generations? And where is the line between keeping a dish’s cultural soul and refining it so much that it becomes something else?

Kartheek’s review: two Dubais

What stood out to me is the contrast between two food worlds in Dubai: high-end imported restaurant brands and theatrical fine dining designed to impress tourists, and the hearty, unpretentious food locals actually eat, including Sudanese street food and Levantine bakeries. Al Awadhi bridges the two by taking nostalgic Khaleeji flavors like date syrup and cardamom and turning them into sophisticated pastry. I also noticed that even though she plates with fine-dining tools, she stresses that sharing food, especially during Ramadan and Eid, is still central to Emirati culture.

Questions it raised for me: How can Emirati chefs cater to a global, Michelin-driven tourist market while keeping the essence of traditional Khaleeji food? And in a city built on imports and expatriates, when do immigrant street food vendors become part of “Emirati” food identity?

What this episode taught us: Emirati food is evolving, not being replaced. Chefs like Al Awadhi start from family recipes and trade-route flavors and refine them, the same balance between tradition and change we saw at Abu Naji and in our interview.
