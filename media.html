Watch and Listen: The Gulf on Screen and in Song

By Tanish

Reading about a culture only gets you so far. To get a feel for the Gulf, I wanted to see its food and hear its music. This post covers the food show our group watched and two songs that show how much the region has changed, from the pearl diving boats to today’s pop charts.

Food show review: Somebody Feed Phil, “Dubai”

In Season 7 of Netflix’s Somebody Feed Phil (2024), host Phil Rosenthal spends an episode eating his way through Dubai. The season premiered on March 1, 2024, and the trailer below shows the Dubai stop alongside the season’s other cities.

Official trailer, Somebody Feed Phil Season 7, Netflix.

The episode ends at Al Qbabh, an Emirati restaurant in Aweer known for seafood served with rice (Frying Pan Adventures). That connects directly to the coastal side of Khaleeji food we wrote about on our region page: fish, rice and spices that arrived by boat.

Dubai’s food scene is mostly global, so one question we kept asking while watching was how much of the episode is actually Emirati food. That ties to our project’s big question about tradition surviving inside a modern, global city.

Music: from pearl boats to pop

Fjiri, the songs of Bahrain’s pearl divers

Fjiri is a music and dance tradition that started in Muharraq, Bahrain, in the 19th century. It grew out of pearl diving and gave divers entertainment and relief from stress during their time on land. Musicians play finger chimes, drums and a clay pot called a jahl, sitting around a circle while a lead singer and dancers perform in the middle. Divers gathered to perform it in meeting houses called durs, and today the Ismeal Dawas band in Muharraq still practices every Wednesday evening. Fjiri was added to UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list in December 2021 (The National).

“Bawada’ak” by Hussain Al Jassmi

Hussain Al Jassmi, from the UAE, is one of the biggest names in modern Khaleeji music, and “Bawada’ak” (2001) was the song that made him a star (The National). Listening to fjiri and then to Al Jassmi is a quick way to hear the jump from the pearling economy to the oil economy: the songs moved from boats and beaches to studios and stadiums.

My takeaway: The show and the songs tell the same story as the food. The old traditions did not disappear when the region modernized. They moved into new places, from a seafood restaurant at the edge of Dubai to a UNESCO list to a pop song.
