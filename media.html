Media: Food Shows and Music

Watch and Listen: The Gulf on Screen and in Song

Food show reviews by Aarav, Kartheek and Tanish · Music by Tanish

Reading about a culture only gets you so far. For our food show reviews, our group watched two episodes of Matbakh, afikra’s series on the food of the Arab world, where host Mikey Muhanna talks with chefs about the history behind their cuisine. One episode covers the UAE and the other covers Bahrain, two of the Khaleeji countries on our region page.

Food show 1: How did the Emirates evolve into a food-loving country?

Matbakh, Episode 8 · Guest: Sahar Parham Al Awadhi · United Arab Emirates · about 48 minutes

Aarav’s review

Sahar Parham Al Awadhi is an Emirati pastry chef who worked at the Burj Al Arab and was named the Middle East and North Africa’s Best Pastry Chef. She explains that the area was historically poor and not agriculturally rich, so its cooking borrowed from Iran, Oman, Saudi Arabia, Yemen and India, with spices arriving along old trade routes. That matched exactly what we found in our own research.

The traditional foods were my favorite part: farni, a runny rice pudding; rangina, fresh dates covered in roasted flour, sugar and cardamom at the end of summer; regag, a thin crispy crepe spread with mahyawa, a fermented fish paste aged in a clay pot; and luqaimat soaked in date syrup. She rates Dubai “a solid eight” as a food city and says it is “forming its own culinary DNA.” She also prefers the word “evolution” over “modernizing” for how Emirati food should change, which became a theme for our whole project.

Questions it raised for me: If Gulf countries share nearly identical dishes under different names, who decides what counts as a “national” cuisine? And as Emirati chefs bring French pastry techniques into traditional sweets, how can they refine these dishes without losing the family memories that give them meaning?

Food show 2: Bahraini cuisine with Tala Bashmi

Matbakh · Guest: Tala Bashmi · Bahrain · about 50 minutes

Aarav’s review: a cuisine built on necessity

Tala Bashmi, named MENA’s Best Female Chef in 2022 and chef patron of Fusions by Tala in Manama, describes Bahraini food as a cuisine born of necessity. Bahrain is a small island with about six good months and six months of dry heat, so it depends on imports. Its people were fishermen and pearl divers, not wealthy, so food had to stretch and last: salted and dried shrimp and fish, and one whole fish shredded over rice so every family member gets a share. That is the same “feed many from little” idea we saw in machboos.

Her chosen ingredient is mehyawa, a fermented fish sauce made from dried fish, coriander, cumin and salt, left in the sun for about a month and eaten on bread with a fried egg and cheese. For celebrations there is ghoozi, baby lamb over rice for Eid, the dish she missed most while studying abroad. As an international student, I related to that a lot.

Questions it raised for me: Bashmi says Bahraini palates were shaped by the Indian, Filipino and Persian workers who cooked in many homes. How should a national cuisine acknowledge the migrant hands that shaped it? And if Bahraini food was built on scarcity and sharing, can it move into luxury fine dining without losing that meaning?

What both episodes taught us: Both chefs describe food shaped by a harsh climate, trade and migration, and both are reinventing it rather than replacing it. That is the same balance between tradition and change we found at Abu Naji and in our interview.

Music: from pearl boats to pop

Fjiri, the songs of Bahrain’s pearl divers

Fjiri is a music and dance tradition that started in Muharraq, Bahrain, in the 19th century. It grew out of pearl diving and gave divers relief during their time on land. Musicians play finger chimes, drums and a clay pot called a jahl, sitting around a circle while a lead singer and dancers perform in the middle. Fjiri joined UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list in December 2021 (The National). It pairs well with Tala Bashmi’s episode: the same pearl divers who sang fjiri ate the dried fish and rice she describes.

“Bawada’ak” by Hussain Al Jassmi

Hussain Al Jassmi, from the UAE, is one of the biggest names in modern Khaleeji music, and “Bawada’ak” (2001) was the song that made him a star (The National). Going from fjiri to Al Jassmi is a quick way to hear the jump from the pearling economy to the oil economy.
